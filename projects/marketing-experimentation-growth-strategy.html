Marketing Experimentation & Growth Strategy

Do promotional emails actually make customers more likely to buy?

Help a retailer choose what to send and use a limited marketing budget wisely.

I used SQL and Python to analyze a real 2008 experiment with 64,000 shoppers: compare three email options, study customer differences, and weigh email-cost and sending-limit scenarios.

The Problem

A retailer needs to know whether an email creates purchases that would not otherwise happen, which apparel campaign to use, and whether more detailed customer selection is worth the extra work.

Someone who is already likely to buy may not need another promotion. The business needs to measure the email’s added effect—not just find likely buyers.

My Approach

  1. Compare three randomly assigned groups: men’s apparel email, women’s apparel email, and no email.
  2. Check the data with SQL and Python, compare purchases and spending, and examine customers’ previous buying habits.
  3. Develop sending strategies using one half of the data, then compare them on the other half. Give competing strategies the same sending limit.

Email costs and profit margins are scenarios, because the dataset does not provide those business inputs.

What the Data Showed

Both email campaigns led to more purchases than no email in this experiment. The men’s apparel email had the highest purchase rate.

  • Men’s apparel: about 13 buyers per 1,000 shoppers. Women’s apparel: about 9. No email: about 6.
  • That is about 7 extra buyers per 1,000 for men’s apparel email versus no email—a historical estimate, not a promise for today.
  • A more complex recipient rule did not prove a reliable improvement over randomly choosing the same number of shoppers for the men’s apparel email.

Purchase rate counts shoppers who bought within two weeks, not the number of orders or how much they spent. Actual campaign profit and long-term customer value were not measured.

Business Recommendation

Start with the men’s apparel campaign as the benchmark in a new experiment. Keep a no-email comparison group, then test a more detailed recipient strategy against random selection sending the same number of emails.

Do not add targeting complexity just because its estimate looks better. Require a current experiment to show enough benefit to justify the extra effort.

The next trial should record actual email costs, profit margins, delivery and unsubscribes, and follow customers for longer than two weeks.

Analytical design

Make the decision traceable to the experiment.

Visible SQL and Python audit customer profiles and assigned outcomes. Campaign comparisons estimate intent-to-treat differences in conversion and spend, using confidence intervals and Holm correction within each three-contrast outcome family. Maximum pre-treatment standardized imbalance is 0.0169.

An arm-stratified, fixed-seed 32,000/32,000 split separates policy learning from evaluation. No treatment, visit, conversion or post-email spend is used as a targeting feature. The six-segment rule and control logistic baseline are fitted on training data; no test-set retuning is performed.

Held-out policy values use Horvitz–Thompson weighting with documented assignment probability 1/3, subtracting the same eligible population’s no-email counterfactual. Paired policy differences use the same test customers. Policy intervals are conditional on the learned rule and omit training instability.

Recommendation and next test

Run the simplest defensible pilot first.

Keep Men’s Email as the campaign benchmark. For strict capacity, random Men is a credible low-complexity comparator; the learned segment rule has not demonstrated superiority. Retain a randomized No Email group and do not present historical estimates as realized business growth.

A current experiment should compare random Men, the frozen segment rule and No Email at equal budget, randomizing within customer strata. Record actual contribution margin, delivery, unsubscribes and a longer value horizon. Size it from a management-approved minimum effect; freeze the primary contrast, multiplicity plan and stop rules before assignment.

What the evidence cannot establish

The data is historical, has no customer IDs, and contains only 578 buyers and two weeks of outcomes. There is no actual email cost, margin, unsubscribe or fatigue measure. Exploratory segment findings and conditional policy intervals do not prove individual uplift, long-run profit or current-market transferability. The retrospective analysis plan is not a prospective preregistration.
