Demand Forecast + Replenishment LP

When deliveries and space are limited, how much should a store order each night?

A replenishment analysis on real store sales: forecast daily demand, plan orders with a linear program, and replay both against 84 days of actual sales.

Try the live demo↗Explore the GitHub project↗

The short answer

Plan all items together when capacity is tight. At the base limits, Holt-Winters + LP cost 7% less than the best-tuned order-up-to rule at the same 98.7% fill rate. When deliveries were tight, it cost 29% less.

With spare capacity, a simple rule on the same forecast does as well or slightly better. The LP is worth it where trucks or space run short.

What I did

I chose the question and the setting (one Walmart store, 50 items, shared delivery and storage limits), then compared six forecasts and three ordering policies on 84 days of real sales. I also reviewed where the LP did not win.

Business problem

Trucks and shelves set the limit, not only the forecast.

Each evening a store orders stock that arrives two days later. Only so many units can be delivered per day and only so many fit in the store, and weekend sales run about 30% above average.

A standard order-up-to rule decides each item on its own and, when the truck is full, cuts every item by the same share. The question is whether planning all items together pays off, and when.

What I did

My Approach

  1. Took the 50 best-selling items with few zero-sales days from one Walmart store's food department (M5 data): two years of real daily sales and shelf prices.
  2. Refit six forecasts every evening and scored them on the last 84 days: naive, seasonal naive, moving average, same-weekday average, exponential smoothing and Holt-Winters.
  3. Built a linear program in PuLP that plans a week of orders for all items under shared delivery and storage limits. Each item's buffer comes from its margin and forecast error through the normal loss function, so there is no service target to tune.
  4. Replayed each policy day by day on real sales and compared it with order-up-to rules at seven service targets, keeping each rule's best. Repeated at six capacity settings and in two more stores.

Recorded sales stand in for demand, and costs and capacities are assumptions. The rules' service targets were picked after seeing the test window, which favors the rules.

Key findings

What the Data Showed

33.8%daily forecast error for Holt-Winters, vs. 39.4% for a 7-day moving average
7%lower total cost than the best-tuned rule at the base limits, at the same 98.7% fill rate
29%lower total cost when deliveries are cut to 95% of average demand
10.5%less margin lost, from about the same number of lost units (617 vs. 614)

What it means

What it means

Optimization pays where capacity binds. Before weekends and holidays, trucks and backroom space are the bottleneck, and that is where planning all items together saved the most.

The optimizer needs a good forecast. Fed with the moving-average forecast, the same LP cost more than the simple rule in 5 of 6 capacity settings.

What did not work

  • A scenario version of the LP, planned against 20 demand paths sampled from past errors, cost $700 vs. $395. A lost sale costs about 600 times more than a day of holding, so stock has to cover rare spikes, and 20 samples rarely include them.
  • With plenty of storage, a rule with a 99.99% target beat the LP, whose buffers assume normally distributed forecast errors.

Methods & limitations

How the numbers were built

Data: M5 competition data (Walmart), store CA_1, department FOODS_3, May 2014 to May 2016. The two store-closed Christmas days were filled from the same weekday a week before and after.

Costs: a lost sale costs 30% of the shelf price, holding costs 25% of unit cost per year, and a delivered unit that does not fit goes back for $0.10. Deliveries are capped at 1.05x average daily demand and storage at 3 days, both shared by all 50 items. All are assumptions.

Checks: forecasts only see data up to each evening, every policy starts from the same stock, and the capacity grid and two more stores test whether the result holds.

Scope & limitations

Recorded sales understate demand when an item was out of stock. One department and one 12-week window; two more stores at the base limits only. Delivery and storage are shared pools, not truck space or shelf space per item.
