AI Infrastructure Value Chain

AI spending flows from cloud platforms into the hardware supply chain. Which layer keeps the profit?

An industry analysis built from public financial statements, for strategy and partnership discussions. It is not stock picking.

The short answer

Profit follows scarcity, not volume. AI chips, memory and the foundry took 94% of the $348B in new yearly operating profit across 16 suppliers. NVIDIA alone took 56%.

Server makers added $81B of revenue but kept only 13¢ of profit per new dollar. The five cloud platforms paying for it now spend 82% of their operating cash flow on capex.

What I did

I chose the question, the eight value-chain layers and 21 companies, then compared revenue growth, operating margins, new operating profit and capex from 2022 to the 12 months ending Q2 2026. Claude substantially assisted the code and data processing; I reviewed the data checks and conclusions.

Business problem

Revenue is rising everywhere. Profit is not.

Five big cloud platforms raised yearly capex from $158B to $566B. That money flows through server makers, chip designers, the foundry, memory, networking, optical and power suppliers. Every layer's revenue grew, so revenue alone cannot show who is really earning from AI.

For a strategy or partnership decision, the useful question is where the profit pool sits and how it is moving, not who has the fastest sales growth.

What I did

My Approach

  1. Map the chain into eight layers and pick 21 companies with comparable public filings, including two China-listed optical module makers.
  2. Pull financial data from SEC EDGAR, rebuild every quarter and align different fiscal years to calendar quarters.
  3. Measure profit with operating profit, because revenue is double counted along a supply chain. Compare new profit, profit per new dollar of revenue and margins by layer.
  4. Check that rebuilt quarters add back to reported years, and spot-check figures against earnings releases.

Company totals are used, not AI-only segments, so the numbers show the direction and size of the shift rather than exact AI profit.

  • Optical grew 2.7x but holds only 2% of the new profit. Inside it, Innolight and Eoptolink captured 82% of the layer's new profit at a 41% operating margin, versus 12% for Coherent, Lumentum and Fabrinet.

What it means

What it means

Where profit lands depends on who holds the bottleneck. Layers with pricing power (GPUs, HBM, leading-edge wafers) captured most of the profit; layers that compete on assembly and delivery grew revenue with thin margins.

The money flows down the chain, but the risk flows back up. Cloud capex is close to what existing cash flow can pay for. If capex growth slows, chips and memory have the most profit to give back, and memory has the most cyclical history.

What I would watch next

  • Capex as a share of operating cash flow approaching 100%.
  • Custom chips from Broadcom and Marvell taking chip-layer profit share from NVIDIA.
  • Whether memory margins are a new level or a cycle peak.
  • Whether optical turns fast growth into a larger share of profit as clusters scale.

Industry analysis, not a recommendation to buy or sell any security.

Methods & limitations

How the numbers were built

Why operating profit: NVIDIA's revenue includes TSMC wafers and Micron memory, and Dell's includes NVIDIA GPUs. Operating profit is not double counted, because each company keeps only its own margin.

Periods: CY2022 is the baseline, before ChatGPT changed AI spending. The latest period is the 12 months ending in calendar Q2 2026, the newest quarter every company had reported.

Checks: 588 fiscal years of rebuilt quarters match reported annual totals within 0.5%; TSMC entries match its 20-F; NVIDIA, Micron and Dell figures match their earnings releases.

Scope & limitations

Company totals, not AI-only segments. Acquisitions lift some growth (AMD with Xilinx, Broadcom with VMware, Coherent). SK hynix, Samsung and Taiwan server makers are not included because they do not file comparable SEC statements. Capex excludes finance leases, so real data-center spending is higher.
