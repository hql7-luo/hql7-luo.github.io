Tariff-Era Sourcing Decision

US tariffs on Chinese cartons are 37.5%. Should a Zhejiang carton maker move its US production?

A strategy case built on public October 2026 data: tariffs, ocean freight, exchange rates, paperboard prices and wages. The company is fictional.

The short answer

Stay in China for now. Over five years China lands 1,000 cartons for $201.7, versus $213.9 from a new Vietnam plant and $238.5 from Mexico.

Spend about $300K to keep a Vietnam option ready, and move if China's tariff climbs to about 48%, roughly 35 points above Vietnam's.

What I did

I chose the question, set the scope (one product, three sites, four tariff scenarios) and reviewed the assumptions and conclusions. The question connects to my 2026 work at a Zhejiang packaging printer and a trading company.

Business problem

Tariffs went up. A new plant is expensive and hard to undo.

A Zhejiang folding-carton maker ships 60 million printed retail boxes a year to a US customer. Since July 2026, Chinese cartons pay a 37.5% US tariff (Section 301 List 3 plus a new forced-labor Section 301 duty), Vietnamese cartons 12.5%, and USMCA-qualifying Mexican cartons nothing.

The customer buys on landed cost. A lower tariff abroad looks attractive, but a new plant, new overhead, paperboard prices and a ramp-up year all count, and the tariffs themselves could move again.

What I did

My Approach

  1. Build a landed-cost model per 1,000 cartons: factory cost, supplier price, freight and US entry fees, tariff and inventory carrying, delivered to the customer's LA-area warehouse.
  2. Fill it with public October 2026 data (tariff rulings, the Drewry freight index, Federal Reserve exchange rates, board price benchmarks, official wage statistics) and label every plant-level assumption.
  3. Compare four strategies over five years: stay in China, move to Vietnam, move to Mexico, or stay and keep Vietnam ready. Test four tariff scenarios and 10,000 simulated futures.
  4. Find the break-even points and the inputs that could change the answer, and check that the Python model and the web explorer give the same numbers.

The company is fictional and plant-level costs are assumptions, so the numbers show the shape of the decision, not a quote.

Key findings

What the Model Showed

$201.7per 1,000 cartons landed from China, vs. $213.9 from Vietnam
~48%China tariff at which Vietnam breaks even (today 37.5%)
2.3×Mexico's paperboard cost vs. China's
78%of 10,000 simulated futures where China stays cheapest
  • Mexico loses on paperboard, not wages. North American board costs about $1,249/t delivered versus $535/t in China, and since July 2026 Mexico charges an antidumping duty of up to $0.49/kg on Chinese board. Mexico would need board near $910/t to match China.

Recommendation

Recommendation

Do not move yet. Keep US production in China and spend about $300K to keep Vietnam ready: shortlist sites, run sample orders with a local converter, and get the customer to pre-approve a Vietnam source.

Move when China's tariff on cartons reaches about 48% with Vietnam at 12.5%. The pending excess-capacity Section 301 case, which names China, Vietnam and the paper sector, is the main way that could happen.

Signposts I would watch

  • The excess-capacity Section 301 decision, and how far it separates China's and Vietnam's rates.
  • The court challenge to the forced-labor Section 301 duties, which could bring China's rate back to 25%.
  • A longer customer commitment: an 8-year payback cuts Vietnam's gap from about $12 to $5 per 1,000.
  • Mexican board below about $910/t, or USMCA preference weakening after the annual review.

Fictional company and a cost-only view; not trade, legal or investment advice.

Methods & limitations

How the numbers were built

Landed cost = factory cost (board, labor, energy, overhead, new-plant payback) + 15% supplier margin + freight and US entry fees + tariff on the customs value + inventory carrying for transit and safety stock.

Five-year view at a 10% discount rate. A new site has a weaker first year and must recover a $5M line plus $1M startup cost. Tariff scenarios: status quo 40%, relief 20%, overcapacity tariffs 30%, USMCA friction 10%; the probabilities are judgment calls.

Checks: 12 automated tests, including that cost components add up, break-even points really equalize costs, and the JavaScript explorer matches Python.

Scope & limitations

The company is fictional; labor hours, overhead, scrap and plant cost are assumptions. Tariffs change quickly and some are in court. The model compares cost only: it leaves out customer relationships, quality risk, the value of Mexico's faster delivery beyond inventory, and local tax incentives.
